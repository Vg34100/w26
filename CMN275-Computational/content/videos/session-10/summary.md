# CMN SESSION 10 - Ethics

## Lecture Highlights

- AI seen as a general‑purpose technology, comparable to electricity or the steam engine.
- Stuart Russell warns that autonomous weapons that can decide to kill threaten security and freedom.
- Machines can amplify human intelligence, but also inherit human biases and limitations.
- AI agents act in the world (e.g., travel‑booking AI) and have agency, unlike static models.
- “Orchestration” = coordinating multiple specialized agents to cover more reality.

## AI Benefits in Education & Work

- Personal AI tutors boost performance by \~2 σ, similar to having a human tutor.
- AI coding assistants (e.g., GitHub Copilot) make senior engineers 55 % faster, raise satisfaction 60‑70 %.
- Bottom‑half performers improve the most with AI tools, acting as an equalizer.
- AI‑generated feedback improves essay scores and motivation.

## AI Creativity & Innovation

- AlphaGo’s Move 37 showed AI can discover novel strategies beyond human prejudice.
- Innovation = recombining existing elements (Schumpeter); AI excels at exploring massive combinatorial spaces.
- AlphaFold solved protein‑folding for millions of proteins, earning a Nobel Prize in Chemistry.

## Ethical Risks & Dilemmas

- Poorly defined goals (reward functions) can lead to catastrophic outcomes (paper‑clip maximizer, King Midas).
- AI can simulate empathy without feeling, likened to psychopaths—risk of emotional manipulation.
- Homogeneity from shared AI tools creates “single‑point‑of‑failure” risks in complex systems.
- Autonomous weapons, AI‑driven surveillance, and AI‑mediated political influence raise security concerns.

## Machine‑Learning Paradigm & Ethics Frameworks

- Data + goal → algorithm (the “master algorithm”) replaces the traditional recipe‑based approach.
- **Virtue ethics** → quality of data (biases, fairness).
- **Deontology** → clarity of the reward/goal function (intent).
- **Consequentialism** → focus on outcomes; unintended side effects must be evaluated.

## Research Ethics (Belmont Report & Regulations)

- **Respect for Persons**: informed consent, comprehension, voluntary participation.
- **Beneficence**: maximize benefits, minimize harms; protect privacy (e.g., anonymize data).
- **Justice**: fair subject selection; justify exclusions (e.g., gender, ethnicity).
- IRB approval required for federally funded or institutionally regulated human‑subjects research.
- Historical abuses (Tuskegee, Guatemala syphilis, Milgram, Stanford Prison) illustrate why safeguards matter.

## Real‑World Cases & Lessons

- Facebook emotional‑contagion study lacked consent → violated Respect for Persons.
- Scraping public Twitter data for research can be ethically ambiguous; context and privacy expectations matter.
- AI‑mediated deliberation (Habermas Machine) outperformed human mediators in capturing public will.
- Character‑AI chatbots can foster unhealthy attachment; emotional dependence may outweigh intended benefits.

## Governance & Future Directions

- Three safety rules suggested by AI pioneers: no open‑internet connection, no exposure to vulnerable users, prevent self‑modifying code.
- Continuous monitoring, humility, and interdisciplinary oversight needed as AI integrates into society.
- Balancing rapid AI progress with robust ethical frameworks is essential to avoid “the road to hell paved with good intentions.”
