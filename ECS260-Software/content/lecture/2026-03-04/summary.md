## Action Items

- Draft a clear research question before designing any survey
- Define the target population and decide on a sampling method (simple random, stratified, cluster, etc.)
- Create a short, simple survey instrument; keep it concise and user‑friendly
- Offer a small incentive (e.g., personalized feedback, modest reward) to boost response rates
- Pilot the survey with a small group, refine based on feedback, then roll out to the full sample

## Trend Lines & Case Studies

- Use case studies to confirm, expand, or contradict existing theories
- Main‑line examples support the theory; off‑trend examples highlight new phenomena
- Analogy: HR diagram in astronomy shows a main sequence plus outliers (dwarfs, giants)
- Treat off‑trend cases as separate insights rather than forcing them onto the main trend
- Selecting both on‑trend and off‑trend cases adds depth to your analysis

## Survey Research Basics

- Surveys collect data from large populations to describe, compare, or explain behaviors
- Ideal for questions where you can’t control variables but need broad insight
- Relies on self‑reported outcomes, so consider truthfulness and bias
- Useful for measuring frequency of a characteristic (e.g., % of companies using Agile)

## Designing Survey Instruments

- Keep questions short, clear, and neutral—avoid leading language
- Order questions thoughtfully; earlier items can influence later answers
- Choose the right response scale (e.g., 1‑5 Likert) and stick to standard formats
- Provide clear instructions and make the survey easy to access (email link, web form)

## Sampling Strategies

- Simple random sampling is common, but stratified, cluster, or multistage designs can improve representation
- Identify the “unit of study” (individuals, teams, organizations) and the “universe” you want to infer about
- Guard against self‑selection bias—ensure a diverse set of participants is invited

## Boosting Response Rates

- Offer something of value (personalized feedback, comparison report, small gift)
- Keep the survey brief and convenient (online link, mobile‑friendly)
- Send reminders and consider multiple invitation channels (email, Slack, in‑app)
- Pilot first to spot confusing items that could lower completion rates

## Comparing Research Methods

- **Case studies:** deep, contextual insight from a few selected cases; ties back to theory
- **Surveys:** broad, quantitative snapshot across many respondents; good for prevalence and correlation
- **Experiments:** test causal effects by manipulating variables
- **Ethnography:** immersive observation of culture and practices; rich qualitative data
