# Introduction to a Graduate-Level Operating Systems Research Course
The instructor provides a comprehensive overview of a graduate-level course on operating systems research, detailing its discussion-based format, learning objectives, and core assignments. The curriculum is designed to give students practice in reading, discussing, and analyzing research papers through student-led presentations and a final, open-ended research project.

------------
## Course Goals and Core Components
The course is a graduate-level seminar focused on research, with Arnav serving as the Teaching Assistant (TA), a key resource for students. The primary objective is to provide students with extensive practice in reading and discussing research papers. To achieve this, the course is built upon several core components: a curated reading list of papers to be covered in class, short quizzes to assess comprehension, and a research project. A central element involves student-led lectures, where groups of three to five students will present a research paper and lead a subsequent class discussion.

------------
## Class Structure and Discussion Format
The instructor emphasizes a discussion-based pedagogy over traditional lectures to foster deeper engagement. A two-phase discussion format will be implemented: following research talks, students will first form small groups to crystallize their thoughts before engaging in a full-class discussion. This format proved effective in previous years and will be utilized again. The course will cover various operating systems topics, with each topic block introduced by the instructor and followed by the reading and discussion of three to four related research papers over one to two weeks.

A dedicated discussion section is scheduled immediately after the main class. During the first half of the quarter, this section will focus on practical skills for systems research, such as delivering research talks. In the latter half, it will transition to a workshop for students to present project updates and receive feedback. While core topics are set, students are encouraged to explore additional areas like virtualization, distributed systems, and security for their projects. Students are expected to read one to two papers per week before class and arrive prepared for discussion.

------------
## Major Assignments and Evaluation
The course evaluation is based on several key assignments, including student-led paper discussions, a semester-long research project, and short quizzes.

*   **Student-Led Paper Discussions**: Student teams will lead the discussion for one research paper, with presentations starting two weeks from the course's start. Preparation involves reading the paper, creating slides, and formulating thoughtful discussion questions. The suggested presentation format is 5 minutes for background, 10 minutes for core ideas, and 10 minutes for discussion. Grades will be based on the polish of the presentation and the depth of the discussion questions.
*   **Research Project**: Students will work in groups on an open-ended research project that ideally advances the state of the art in operating systems. Projects can range from measurement studies to recreating existing systems. Key milestones include a project proposal and a final presentation during the last weeks of the quarter. The final paper is due on March 18th, with no late submissions accepted.
*   **Quizzes and Grading**: There will be eight short quizzes throughout the term, with the lowest four scores dropped. Each quiz is worth four points. The course uses a straight grading scale where 93% and above corresponds to an A, and all assignment point values are published on Canvas.
*   **Academic Integrity and AI Policy**: Collaboration on understanding papers is encouraged. However, any borrowed resources for projects must be properly cited. The use of AI tools for writing or code generation is permitted, but students are required to perform significant human revisions and are fully responsible for the final work's correctness and integrity.

------------
## The Rationale and Scope of Operating Systems Research
The course underscores the value of studying operating systems, as this knowledge is foundational for understanding program resource needs, memory management, and lower-level systems programming. An operating system is defined as the software layer that mediates between computer hardware and user applications, providing abstractions like virtual memory and files to simplify programming and manage resources.

The instructor highlights that the field is rich with research opportunities due to fundamental shifts in hardware trends. The end of Moore's Law and Dennard scaling has halted the automatic increase in single-core CPU speeds, leading to the rise of multi-core processors and specialized hardware like GPUs. These changes, combined with the emergence of complex, high-performance applications (e.g., large-scale machine learning, distributed databases), create a need to redesign operating systems to manage modern hardware and workloads efficiently. Systems research is characterized by combining existing techniques in novel ways and navigating complex trade-offs to solve new problems.

------------
## Methodology for Reading Technical Papers
The instructor provides a structured methodology for reading dense technical papers, advising against a linear, page-one-to-end approach. Instead, a multi-pass strategy is recommended for progressively deeper comprehension.

*   **First Pass**: The initial pass is for gaining a high-level understanding. This involves reading the title, abstract, introduction, subheadings, and conclusion, as well as scanning the references. The goal is to categorize the paper, identify the problem it addresses, and understand its core contributions to decide if a deeper read is warranted.
*   **Second Pass**: This pass involves a more thorough reading of the paper's text, with a focus on understanding the key ideas and their impact. Students should pay close attention to graphs and figures to see how the authors support their claims. The objective is to grasp the solution and the evidence for its effectiveness.
*   **Third Pass**: The final pass is for a deep, critical analysis. At this stage, the reader aims to understand the implementation details well enough to hypothetically recreate the work. This involves identifying potential shortcomings, questioning assumptions, and brainstorming ideas for future extensions or improvements. This critical engagement is supported by asking detailed questions about the problem, solution, evaluation methods, and overall impact.
