## Paper Information

**Title:** More Than Telling a Story: Transforming Data into Visually Shared Stories

**Authors:** Bongshin Lee, Nathalie Henry Riche, Petra Isenberg, Sheelagh Carpendale

**Publication:** IEEE Computer Graphics and Applications, 2015

## Summary Sources

From page 84, the abstract frames the full process as "transforming data into visually shared stories" with steps for "explore data," "make a story," and "tell a story." This is the backbone of the summary, and it is restated again in the Figure 2 caption on page 86 where they call these the three main components. I used those lines to structure the summary around a three part process.

The paper also says the process does not have to move left to right and can include loops, which appears on page 86. That is why I said it is not strictly linear and can go back when more evidence or better excerpts are needed.

## Question 1: Components of the storytelling process

For the first component, page 86 says exploring data involves activities centered around exploring and analyzing data. That is why I described it as collecting excerpts, facts, and evidence from the dataset. For the second component, page 86 says the excerpts are assembled into a storyline and that the activities include ordering, logical connections, flow, and message. That is where I got the idea that making a story is about building a plot and shaping the message. For the third component, page 87 explains telling a story as building a presentation, sharing it, and receiving feedback. That is why I described story material and delivery to an audience as part of that stage.

---

## Question 2: Impact of audience and setting

Page 87 says considerations about the target audience are taken into account, which is why I said the audience affects every stage. The same page says the setting can be described by time and place and the level of audience participation. I used that to explain the difference between live settings and asynchronous settings, and why it changes pacing and the kind of guidance needed. The paper also notes that the medium affects how story material is created and presented, which supports the point that static images, videos, and interactive systems all lead to different storytelling choices.

---

## Question 3: Museum touch table versus New York Times webpage

This comparison comes from the settings described in Table 1 on page 87. The table lists different combinations of time, place, and audience participation. A museum touch table is synchronous and colocated with high participation, which lines up with settings like dynamic discussions. A New York Times data story is asynchronous and distributed with low participation, which is closer to static infographics or recorded videos. That difference in setting and participation is why I said the story material and guidance needs to change, even if the overall three step process stays the same.
